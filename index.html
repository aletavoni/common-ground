A QUICK FIELD GUIDE

How the game works

You play with three bots. The group always contains two players with 120 units and two with 80. Your starting budget is your total budget for all ten rounds.

  1. Make a choice each round. Invest whole units in public protection, private protection, or both. Spend at most 20 in total that round and never more than you have left. You can spend zero.
  2. Contributions accumulate. Group public contributions of at least 160 protect everyone. Your own private contributions of at least 60 protect only you. Investments cannot be withdrawn or refunded, even if both targets are met.
  3. After round 10, calculate payoffs. If either target protects you, your payoff is your unspent money. Otherwise, it is zero. There is no random disaster draw.

What does “Nash equilibrium” mean?

A set of plans where no player can earn more by changing their own plan while everyone else keeps theirs. It does not have to be the best outcome for the group.

Private plan: everyone pays 60 for themselves. Each player would need 160 to provide the public solution alone, more than either starting budget. Spending 60 privately is therefore a best response. Final payoffs are 60, 60, 20 and 20: 160 together.

Public plan: everyone pays 40 to the shared solution. If the others still pay 120, you need to add 40 to reach 160. Choosing private protection instead costs 60. Contributing 40 is therefore a best response. Final payoffs are 80, 80, 40 and 40: 240 together.

What the bots do

These are two selected pure-strategy Nash equilibrium profiles of the full ten-round game. Private bots pay 20 privately in each of rounds 1–3; public bots pay 4 publicly in every round. They make their choices simultaneously with you and never react to your current choice or past choices. If you deviate, the resulting play need not be an equilibrium.

The fixed plans are not claimed to be subgame-perfect strategies: they need not be optimal after every possible history. Other Nash equilibria, including other ways of sharing the public cost, exist.

Playing with unknown bot plans

Choose “What will the others do?” to play without knowing the bots’ plan in advance. After you confirm your choice, a 50/50 draw selects one plan for all three bots. Under the private plan, each bot invests up to 20 toward its own 60-unit protection target. Under the public plan, each bot invests up to 4 in the shared solution. Bots cannot spend more than they have left; a bot that has already reached its private target contributes zero on a private draw. The next round gets a fresh, independent draw with the same odds.

After each round, you see the plan drawn, what each bot contributed and the updated group public total. Earlier draws stay visible in the round history but don’t affect the next draw. The final comparison is a hindsight benchmark based on the bots’ realized public contributions, not a strategy you could follow knowing those totals in advance.

Research & teaching choices

Based on Malthouse et al. (2026), The private solution trap in collective action problems across 34 nations, PNAS 123, e2504632123, especially pp. 3 and 9.

The app keeps the paper’s group sizes, endowments, ten rounds, combined 20-unit round limit, targets and payoff rule. This teaching version lets you select your endowment and uses whole units. The two known-plan versions disclose bot schedules. In the unknown-plan version, an independent 50/50 draw selects the private or public strategy each round and reveals the bots’ contributions after you confirm your choice. It omits the effort task, luck/merit treatments and research questionnaires. In the known-plan versions, as in the study, only your own investments and group public contributions are shown during play; individual bot totals appear at the end. The unknown-plan version also shows how much each bot spent in the just-completed round; its round-by-round randomization is an additional teaching adaptation. The equilibrium examples are derived for this app, not estimates of participants’ behavior or national differences.

Everything runs in your browser. No choices or personal details are sent to a game server. Downloading results saves a CSV on your device. The hosting service may maintain ordinary access logs.
